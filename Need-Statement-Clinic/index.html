Need Statement Clinic
You watched a case at BMC. Now turn what you saw into a need your team can actually design against — and learn to spot the six ways need statements fail before you write your own.
The chain: observation → problem → need
Each link answers a different question. Skipping straight from what you saw to what you will build is the most common way teams go wrong.
Observation
What did you see?
A specific thing that happened, recorded without interpretation. One case, one moment, one workaround.
“The nurse re-taped the same tube three times in one shift; the patient was sweating.”
Problem
What is going wrong, and why does it matter?
The pattern behind the observations, plus its consequence for the patient or the clinician. A problem may describe current practice.
“Tape loses grip on wet skin, so the tube drifts from its placed depth.”
Need
What must be achieved — for whom, and measured how?
A solution-neutral statement of the outcome to be reached. Many different designs should be able to satisfy it.
“A way to keep an endotracheal tube at its placed depth in ICU adults with wet facial skin, measured by change in tube depth over 24 hours.”
The three parts (BE695 format)
Every need statement you submit in this course has all three, and embeds no solution.
01
Problem
The clinical state to be achieved or resolved — not a solution, and not a category like “outcomes.”
02
Population
Which patients, which clinicians, which setting. “Patients” alone is not a population.
03
Measurable outcome
Something you could count or measure to know whether the need was met.
A way to keep the two sternal halves in stable apposition through bone healing in adults with high body mass after median sternotomy, measured by sternal instability at follow-up.
Notice what is absent: no wire, no plate, no band, no cable. A dozen different mechanisms could satisfy this statement — which is exactly the point.

Where these rules come from
Keep these three kinds of claims separate. Confusing them is how teams end up arguing that a regulator requires something no regulator has ever mentioned.
Required by regulation
A manufacturer must establish user needs and design and development inputs, and those inputs must include functional, performance, usability and safety requirements according to the device's intended use. In the US this sits under FDA's Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR), which took effect February 2, 2026 and incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference; design and development inputs are ISO 13485:2016 §7.3.3. Neither the regulation nor the standard prescribes how a need statement should be worded.
Common practice, not required
Writing needs as a single solution-neutral sentence with a problem, a population and an outcome is a convention of device design education and practice — the form BE695 uses is adapted from the Stanford Biodesign process. Scoping a need up and down a ladder of specificity, and separating observation from problem from need, come from the same tradition. Useful and widely taught. Not a legal requirement.
BE695 course convention
In this course: the three-part format is required in your deliverables; a need statement must not embed a solution; and the six failure modes named above are the vocabulary we use in review. The all-or-nothing tagging in this app is a teaching device, not a claim that reviewers grade this way in industry.

About the examples. Every outcome measure in this app is illustrative — a placeholder that shows what “measurable” looks like, not a clinical target, a published figure, or a recommended endpoint. No incidence rates or statistics are quoted anywhere, and none should be inferred. Clinical settings are drawn from BE695 device areas (embolic protection, endotracheal tubes, dialysis catheters, sternal closure, drug delivery, surgical adhesions) and simplified for teaching.

If you are stuck
Go back down the chain. Write the observation first — one specific thing you saw, with no interpretation. Then write the problem: what pattern that observation belongs to, and what it costs the patient or the clinician. The need is what is left when you strip the problem of every assumption about how it gets fixed. If you cannot state an outcome you could measure, you are probably still describing a category (“better recovery”) rather than a problem.
